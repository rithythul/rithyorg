In 2011, I started smallworld with more enthusiasm than experience. Thirteen years later, we have built KOOMPI, launched Selendra, created VitaminAir, and helped over 50 startups. This is what worked, and what didn't.

Early on, from 2011 to 2013, I thought startups just needed money. We focused on connecting entrepreneurs with investors. Most failed anyway. Money without mentorship, market understanding, and discipline in execution is just expensive education.

We tried importing accelerator models that worked in San Francisco. They failed in Phnom Penh: different infrastructure, different mindset, different timeline. What grows in California soil doesn't transplant well to Cambodian conditions.

"Move fast and break things" broke more than it built. In Cambodia's relationship-based business culture, broken trust is expensive. Patience is not only a virtue here; it is strategy.

What worked was different. smallworld the co-working space created more value than smallworld the venture fund. Entrepreneurs needed somewhere to work, meet, and feel part of something bigger. Infrastructure came before investment.

KOOMPI started as a computer company and became an education mission. We have now set up 63 computer labs in schools. Training the next generation turned out to matter more than serving the current one.

Selendra is built for local needs with global technology.

We plan for 5 to 10 years and execute in 90-day cycles. Cambodia's bureaucracy and infrastructure require patience, but opportunities move fast.

Three lessons came hard. First, you can't force a startup scene into existence. You can create the conditions, spaces, events, and connections, and then wait for it to grow. What gets built shows quick results but rarely lasts. What grows takes time but endures.

Second, Cambodian developers, designers, and entrepreneurs are capable of world-class work. The limit is not skill; it is confidence and access to markets. Our job is to remove barriers, not to import solutions.

Third, Silicon Valley optimizes for explosive growth and exits. In Cambodia, we optimize for sustainable businesses that employ people and solve real problems. Different measures of success need different strategies.

After 13 years, I am convinced Cambodia's tech future is not about becoming the next Singapore or Vietnam. It is about becoming the first Cambodia: using our own strengths in tourism, agriculture, and manufacturing while building digital skills.

The kids in those 63 KOOMPI labs will build Cambodia's next tech companies. Our job is to give them tools and get out of their way.

Sometimes the best thing an entrepreneur can do is plant trees they will never sit under. In Cambodia, we are still planting.
